The short version: training a model has a huge environmental cost compared with a single prompt. But inference, actually using the models, adds up at today's scale, and over a model's lifetime it can outweigh the training.

I first wrote this up for work, to get my thoughts straight on AI, energy and the environment. This version adds a calculator so you can try out your own usage.

Two easy-to-read pieces from 2025 shaped most of my thinking: MIT's [Explained: Generative AI's environmental impact](https://news.mit.edu/2025/explained-generative-ai-environmental-impact-0117) and Epoch AI's [How much energy does ChatGPT use?](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/how-much-energy-does-chatgpt-use).

## Training uses a huge amount of energy

<StickyNote color="amber" label="Why GPT-3?">
It's from 2020, ancient in AI terms, but it's one of the few big models with published training figures. The big closed labs don't share them any more.
</StickyNote>

MIT cites an estimate of **1,287 MWh** to train GPT-3, producing around **552 tonnes of CO₂**. That's the yearly electricity of about 120 US homes, or nearly 500 UK ones.

Newer models are far bigger. Epoch's data suggests the largest training runs now draw over 100 MW. A run that size lasting a few months would use something like 200 GWh, enough for around 75,000 UK homes for a year.

## How much energy one prompt uses

<StickyNote color="rose" label="The old number">
You'll still see 3 Wh per query quoted, from a 2023 estimate. Epoch thinks it was too high even then, and models and chips have got more efficient since.
</StickyNote>

Epoch estimates a typical GPT-4o query uses around **0.3 Wh**, about a 10W LED bulb running for two minutes.

Google has since [measured its own](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/infrastructure/measuring-the-environmental-impact-of-ai-inference): about 0.24 Wh for a typical Gemini text prompt, counting idle machines and cooling as well as the chips. It also says that figure fell 33 times over the year to May 2025. Nobody outside Google has checked the numbers, but they land close to Epoch's.

Energy per query climbs quickly with longer input, because the model has to work through everything you give it. Input is measured in tokens, chunks of text that average out at roughly three quarters of a word each:

| Query | Energy | Same as a 10W LED for |
| ----- | ------ | --------------------- |
| Short chat | 0.3 Wh | 2 minutes |
| ~10k tokens in, a short paper | 2.5 Wh | 15 minutes |
| ~100k tokens in, about 200 pages | 40 Wh | 4 hours |

Reasoning models and agents push it further. In Epoch's testing, o1 produced around 2.5 times as many tokens as GPT-4o for the same question, and an agent can make dozens of calls for one request.

## Inference never stops

<StickyNote color="sky" label="Rough maths">
2.5 billion × 0.3 Wh = 750 MWh a day. 1,287 MWh ÷ 750 MWh ≈ 1.7 days. Treat 750 MWh as a floor: it ignores long inputs, reasoning, images, and every AI service that isn't ChatGPT.
</StickyNote>

Training is a one-off. Inference runs continuously across millions of users for as long as the model is in service, and MIT notes it's a growing share of AI's energy use.

In July 2025, OpenAI told Axios that ChatGPT was handling about **2.5 billion prompts a day** ([via TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/21/chatgpt-users-send-2-5-billion-prompts-a-day/)). Even if every one were a short chat, that's as much electricity as training GPT-3 roughly every two days.

## Electricity isn't the whole cost

MIT also covers what a per-prompt figure misses. Cooling uses roughly two litres of water for every kWh. Making GPUs and building data centres has its own footprint too, and there's more hardware every year: GPU shipments to data centres rose from 2.67 million in 2022 to 3.85 million in 2023.

Where the power comes from matters as well. Going by [Ember's grid data](https://ember-energy.org/data/yearly-electricity-data/), the same kWh on France's mostly nuclear grid produces about a tenth of the CO₂ it would in the US.

MIT's early-2025 article also cites a projection that data centres could use around 1,050 TWh in 2026, which is this year. That now looks far too high. The IEA's [2026 report](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary) puts 2025 at about 485 TWh, up 17% on the year before, and doesn't expect data centres to reach 950 TWh until 2030. The AI-focused part is growing much faster, though: up 50% in 2025.

## My takeaway

Total use is still climbing, but I only see the cost per task going down: chips, models and data centres keep getting more efficient, and cleaner grids make each kWh cost less CO₂.

So it isn't something I go out of my way to cut back on. AI is a big part of my day-to-day work now, and I think it's worth the energy it uses. Most people don't weigh up the carbon cost of jumping on a flight for a holiday, and even if you do, you're nowhere near the worst offender (looking at you, Taylor Swift :airplane:).

It does have an impact, and it's worth making good choices for the environment overall. But I think the "AI is bad for the environment" story is overblown. A year of 100 short prompts a day comes out at about the same CO₂ as driving 26 km. Oil spills, single-use plastics and companies like Shell do far more damage.

A lot of this gets framed as personal responsibility, but what one person does with their prompts barely moves the needle. The big companies and governments are the ones who can actually fix it.
